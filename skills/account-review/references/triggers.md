# Expansion triggers

Record an opportunity only when a trigger actually fired AND Sobieski can perform
the work. A trigger without a service behind it is a note, not an opportunity.

## 1. Open deficiencies (the big one)

Every open deficiency is work that was already found, already documented, and in
most cases already quoted. It is the shortest path to revenue in the business and
the most commonly dropped.

For each: what it is, date found, severity, whether it was quoted, whether the
quote was ever sent, last customer response.

Age them. A deficiency open past 90 days usually means the follow-up stopped, not
that the customer said no. Those are worth a call, not a re-quote.

Separate the ones the customer explicitly declined. Those are a different
conversation and re-pitching them without new information damages the account.

## 2. Cycle items with no record

From `references/cycles.md`. An item with no performance record at all usually
means it was never part of the contract. Five-year internal obstruction
investigations and fire pump flow tests are the two that turn up most often.

This is the cleanest expansion play available because it is not a pitch. The work
is owed regardless of who performs it.

## 3. Services not under contract

Sobieski inspects sprinkler, alarm, extinguishers, and pumps. Most accounts buy
some subset.

If a building has a fire alarm system and Sobieski only holds the sprinkler
contract, someone else is inspecting the alarm, or nobody is. Both are
opportunities and the second is urgent.

Check every system present in the building against every service under contract.

## 4. Buildings not on the contract

Portfolio customers add, buy, and lease buildings. The contract does not follow
automatically. Compare the building list against the contract schedule.

## 5. Equipment at end of life

Aging alarm panels, obsolete devices with no replacement parts, extinguishers
past economical repair, backflows that fail repeatedly. These are projects, not
service line items, and they carry the largest dollar values in a review.

Flag the condition and what it implies. Do not size the project without a survey.

## 6. Occupancy or use change

A change of occupancy classification can change what is required. Tenant
build-outs, a change from office to residential, added storage height, new
commercial kitchen. Any of these can trigger new requirements.

Worth asking about directly at renewal. Customers rarely think to mention it.

## 7. AHJ pressure

A citation, a red tag, a failed inspection, or a letter from the fire marshal
compresses every timeline. Work that sat quoted for a year moves in a week.

If there is AHJ correspondence in the file, that account goes to the top of the
list regardless of every other score.

## 8. Contact and relationship change

A new property manager or facility director resets the relationship. It is both
the highest churn risk in the business and the best moment for a walkthrough,
because the new person needs to understand the building and has no history with
the incumbent.

## Recording an opportunity

For each, capture: account, building, trigger, what the work is, whether it needs
a quote from `fire-inspection-quote`, rough value band, urgency, and what the
next action actually is.

The next action is a specific thing Shawn does, with a name attached. "Call
Maria about the eleven open deficiencies from the March annual." Not "follow up."
