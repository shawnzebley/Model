# MaxIntel research

A curated directory of open-source research tools, kept at
[maxintel.org](https://maxintel.org/index.html). The links themselves live in
`references/sources.md` — read that file when you need to pick a tool.

This skill exists because general web search is the wrong instrument for a lot
of research. Search engines index what is public, current, and popular. Much of
what verifies a claim is public but unindexed: registry filings, license
databases, archived versions of pages, image provenance. The directory is a map
of those places.

## When to reach for this

- Checking a company, property, or person before a call, a quote, or a deal.
- Verifying a specific claim — that a business is registered, a license is
  current, an address is what someone says it is, a person holds the role they
  claim.
- Tracing an image: where it came from, whether it is original, whether it has
  been used elsewhere.
- A general web search has stalled and the answer likely sits in a database,
  registry, or archive rather than on a web page.

## How to work

**Start from the question, not the tool.** Write down what would actually settle
the matter before opening anything. "Is this contractor licensed in this state"
points at a state license lookup; "is this company real" points at a secretary
of state registry. Picking the tool first tends to produce a pile of tabs and no
answer.

**Prefer the authoritative source.** An aggregator is a starting point, not a
citation. When an aggregator turns something up, go find the primary record it
drew from and cite that instead — registries, filings, and license databases are
the record; a site that repackages them is a copy that may be stale.

**Corroborate before relying on it.** One source is a lead. Two independent
sources that do not draw from the same upstream database are a finding. Watch
for the case where several sites agree because they all scraped the same feed —
that is one source wearing several hats.

**Record where each fact came from.** Note the URL and the date you retrieved
it. Records change and pages disappear; a finding you cannot re-trace in a month
is not much of a finding.

**Note what you could not confirm.** An honest gap is more useful than a
confident guess. Say plainly which parts of the picture are verified and which
are inference.

## Reverse image search

No single engine is good enough alone — they index different corners of the web
and are each strong in different places. Run the image through more than one:

- **Google Lens** — broad coverage, good at objects, landmarks, products, and
  text inside the image.
- **Yandex** — consistently the strongest at faces and at places, and often
  finds matches the others miss entirely. Worth running even when the others
  come back empty.
- **TinEye** — sorted by oldest first, which is the one that answers "where did
  this actually first appear." Weak on similar-but-not-identical images, strong
  on exact copies.
- **Bing Visual Search** — different index again; cheap to add as a third pass.

Technique that matters more than the engine choice:

- **Crop to the distinctive part and search again.** A full frame gives the
  engine too much to match on. A sign, a logo, a building facade, a piece of
  equipment, a nameplate — cropped and searched on its own — routinely finds
  what the whole image does not.
- **Sort by oldest, not best.** For provenance the question is which copy came
  first, and the best-looking match is usually a later repost.
- **Check the file's own metadata before searching.** EXIF may carry the camera,
  the timestamp, and sometimes GPS coordinates, which can settle the question
  without any search at all. Note that most social platforms strip EXIF on
  upload, so its absence proves nothing.
- **Read the image itself.** Signage, license plates, storefronts, uniforms,
  vegetation, the angle of shadows, and the language on nearby text all narrow
  down place and time, and often give you the search term that works.
- **A no-match is not proof of originality.** It means the indexes you tried do
  not have it. Say that, rather than concluding the image is original.

## Scope

Use this for legitimate due diligence: business verification, professional
background checks, property and licensing research, confirming a claim before
acting on it. Work from public records and public sources.

Do not use it to build a profile of a private individual who has no bearing on a
business matter, to locate someone who does not want to be found, or to gather
material for harassment. If a research request starts drifting that way, say so
rather than quietly continuing.
